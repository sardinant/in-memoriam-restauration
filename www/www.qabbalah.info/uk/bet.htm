Origins

The origins of the Kabbalah movement go back to ancient times. Some of its component parts can even be found in the apocalyptic literature of the 2nd and 1st centuries BC. The Essenians have sometimes been considered the precursors of the Kabbalah. The Sefer ha-Zohar, the sacred book of the Kabbalists, claims to describe the teaching that Shimon bar Yohai dispensed to his disciples in the 2nd century AD. The Sefer ha-Yetzirah, which was rediscovered in the 9th century, was written between the 3rd and 6th centuries; it already contains the elements of a cosmogony based on the 10 numbers (the 10 Sefirot) and 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet and proposes the Great Name of God as the supreme goal of meditation.


Development

As from the 13th century, the Kabbalists appeared as a distinct mystical group, with such names as Abraham Abulafia, the representative of ecstatic Kabbalism who drew his inspiration from both the Sefer ha-Yetzira and the work of the Jewish philosopher Maimonides (1135-1204), and especially Moses de Leon, who between 1270 and 1280 wrote a series of Aramean treatises forming the Sefer ha-Zohar or Book of Splendour: the importance of this major book of the Kabbalah was such that the entire doctrine has often been confused with this particular work.

In 1492, the Jews were driven out of Spain by Isabella the Catholic, and a group of mystics settled in Safed, a small town of Galilee where for more than a century, Kabbalistic activities were pursued at an intense pace. Moses Cordovero and especially Isaac Luria brought the Kabbalah from the close circle of initiates to a much wider audience. Their doctrine is an attempt to overcome the exile by aggravating the torment but by praising the hope of redemption. The emphasis is placed on the real role played by the Law: to guide Man's inner effort towards the restoration of universal harmony.

One century later, the Kabbalah gave rise to the heretical doctrine led by Sabbatai Zevi, who proclaimed himself Messiah and generated an immense feeling of hope in the Jewish world. In 1666, he converted to Islam, but despite abandoning his previous religious beliefs, he still has his disciples today that see him as personifying the old conception of the Messiah tortured by the forces of evil.

Although Kabbalism still has a few followers, it has today given way to another form of mysticism, known as Hasidism (from hassid, meaning "pious"), which was reformed in Eastern Europe towards the middle of the 18the century, but which inherited a lot of elements from the ancient mysticism.

Kabbalistic thought, which is essentially purely Jewish, broke through into the Christian world. It obviously did not escape the notice of the Cathars. Raymond Lulle (1233-1315), Pic de La Mirandole (1463-1494), Reuchlin (1455-1522) and Paracelse (1493-1541) drew their inspiration from it, as did many writers.
