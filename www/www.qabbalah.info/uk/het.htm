The Torah

In Hebrew, torah means Law. According to the Jewish tradition, the will of God was revealed to Moses upon Mount Sinai when the Covenant was made. The Torah shows how to apply the commandments of this Law.
The Torah is part of the Hebrew Bible and contains the first five of its 24 books: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. It is also called Pentateuch by the Greeks and corresponds to what Christians call the Old Testament.
It is presented as scrolls of parchment, which are venerated by the Jews who consider them as Holy Books. Every year, the Torah is celebrated on the day of Simhat Torah, "the joy of the Law".



The Talmud

Talmud means "teaching" in Hebrew. The Talmud refers to the compilation of oral explanations given to Moses on the Torah (Mishna) and their comments (Gemara).
This book has continually preserved the unity of the Jewish Diaspora and largely exceeds the framework of laws with philosophical reflections, scientific observations, accounts and all sorts of stories from popular life. The Jerusalem Talmud was written towards 250 AD, whereas the Babylonian Talmud was completed at the turn of the 5th century. The latter is more comprehensive and is now considered the authority in Judaism.


Sefer ha-Yetzirah

The Yetzirah, the "Book of Creation", is one of the first actual Kabbalistic works; this rather short text appeared between the 2nd and 6th centuries. It is quoted by various authors, but its origins remain a mystery. It exposes the fundamental concepts of the Sefirot and the Paths, and presents language as the constructor of the real.


Sefer ha-Bahir

The Bahir, the "Book of Illumination", allegedly appeared in Provence, France, at the end of the 12th century. It is presented as a mixture of texts written in Hebrew and Aramean in the form of parables and comments. This manuscript was pieced together from fragments and stands as a compilation of oriental Gnostic sources. It addresses the male and female principles and the problem of evil.


Sefer ha-Zohar

The Zohar, the "Book of Splendour", is considered to be the third Holy Book in Judaism and represents a key work of the Kabbalah. It is attributed to Moses de Leon and dates back to the 13th century. It is a series of comments on the Torah. As with the Yetzirah, it endeavours to describe the divine attributes, the Sefirot. This highly complex book brilliantly illustrates the complexity of the degrees of meaning. Most of the Kabbalah's key concepts are exposed, particularly that God is made inaccessible, unknowable and indescribable through his concealment and infinite dimension (En-Soph); only his attributes can be understood.
